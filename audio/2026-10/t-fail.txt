T4. Failure modes.

Agents mostly break on audio, identity and integration.

The published evidence points to twelve failure modes, and each has a known mitigation.

When a voice agent fails on a real call, the language model is seldom where the trouble starts. A television in the room is transcribed as the caller. The agent hears its own echo and answers it. A phone number is transcribed correctly and still reaches the tool as a different value.

The evidence is uneven, and each row of the table says where it comes from: vendor benchmarks, a public echo test by webrtcHacks, and research papers.

The twelve failures cluster in the audio path, the identity check and the writes to other systems. Every evidence cell names its source, and vendor figures are labeled as vendor-reported.

Background noise and chatter. A TV, traffic or other people in the room get transcribed as the caller, which causes inserted words, false turns and false barge-ins. In a benchmark by ai-coustics, a speaker-isolation vendor, background speech pushed streaming speech-to-text error rates to between 22 percent and 31 percent. Isolating the main speaker cut them by up to 43 percent, mostly by removing inserted words. Vendor-reported. Isolate the caller's voice before speech-to-text and voice activity detection (VAD). Generic noise suppression is best avoided, because it can distort the caller's speech and raise errors.

Wrong speaker answered. A caregiver or bystander answers a verification question, or the agent follows the wrong voice. Research on speaker-conditioned VAD, including Google's Personal VAD, describes detectors that pick out the target speaker frame by frame and ignore others. Diarize the call, which means separating the voices, lock onto the caller's voice in the first seconds, and verify again if the voice changes before any sensitive step.

Self-reply to echo. The agent's own speech leaks back, is transcribed as the caller and gets answered. A public test by webrtcHacks over WebRTC, the browser standard for real-time audio, showed this, and the rates varied widely by client. Use strong echo cancellation, and compare the incoming audio against the agent's own playback.

False barge-in. The agent hears its own echo, takes it for the caller interrupting and stops mid-word. In the same test, a turn-detection model judged a burst of echo to be a completed turn, with a probability of 0.954. Attribute each sound to a speaker, or to echo, before turn detection runs.

VAD threshold trade-off. A lower threshold speeds up replies but admits short bursts of echo. In the webrtcHacks test, cutting the threshold from 500 milliseconds to 384 milliseconds saved 116 milliseconds and added 3 failures. Tune the threshold for each channel, and validate it on real devices.

Misroute. The router picks the wrong sub-agent, or only one of a caller's two intents. In a research paper, the best models reached only 53.4 percent on a multi-intent benchmark. Give the router multi-label output and thresholds, with a frontier model as the fallback.

Context-flipped decision. Extra, benign text in the context changes a routing decision that was correct. In the JevOut study of decision models, natural context flipped 61.4 percent of correct decisions. Route on the caller's turn only, never on retrieved or third-party text.

Entity drift. The transcript is correct, but a different value reaches the tool. A Retell post that cites Cekura Bench documents one instance, a mismatched phone number. Read the value back to the caller for confirmation, and validate it on the server.

Wrong saved data. The agent writes fields incorrectly. In Cekura's speech-to-speech benchmark, one model saved every field correctly on 64.5 percent of data-saving calls, against 92.0 percent for another. Audit write-backs for exact matches.

Dead air. A stalled provider or a stalled reasoning step leaves 10 seconds or more of silence. In the same benchmark, dead air occurred on 3.3 percent to 5.3 percent of calls, depending on the model. Make tool calls asynchronous, use filler speech, and set timeouts that end in a handoff.

Connection failures. The call never completes cleanly. On Cekura Bench, the share of clean calls was as low as 72.4 percent. Build in telephony redundancy, and count these failures against reliability.

Misheard identity details. Names, emails and IDs are garbled, so verification fails and the errors cascade. Sierra reports that context-aware speech recognition cut major errors by up to 15 percent. Vendor-reported. Bias recognition toward the record on file, and spell the details back to the caller.

Two of the twelve failures, the misroute and the context-flipped decision, belong to the router. T5 compares the models that can do that job.
