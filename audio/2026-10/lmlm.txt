Section 9: What comes next.

The next step is a model that looks facts up.

The research on such models is early, but the fact store they depend on is worth building now.

Zhao and colleagues proposed the Limited Memory Language Model (LMLM) in a paper at the ICLR 2026 conference. It is trained so that specific facts, such as a plan's coverage or a card's limit, live in an external database and not in the model's weights. During training the model is not rewarded for memorizing those values; it learns to issue a lookup and copy the answer. Language and reasoning stay in the model, and facts stay in a table that people can inspect, correct and delete.

The approach suits slow-changing knowledge such as benefits, policies and fees. Live data such as balances, claims and appointment slots stays on tool and MCP calls. The demo below deletes a customer's record and shows what each kind of model then says.

Once the record is deleted, the limited-memory model can no longer give the answer, while the standard model goes on repeating it from its weights. The demo mirrors the paper's unlearning result. Removing records from the store made the model forget them with no loss of general ability, where gradient-based unlearning, which adjusts a model's weights to make it forget, degraded it. Illustrative.

A governed fact store fits regulated conversations.

Once facts sit in a store they become governed data: each one is a row with access control, versioning and change review. Every answer has provenance, because each lookup is a logged call with the value it returned. Deletion from the model is real, since removing rows forgets them without retraining. Copies in logs, transcripts, caches and backups still need their own deletion and retention rules.

Less sensitive data sits in the weights, which matters because researchers have extracted names, phone numbers and emails from model weights, even from data seen once. There is also room to shrink the model. In research, a limited-memory model with 382 million parameters came close to a 7 billion model on factual precision; the details are in the technical appendix (T6).

The fact store is the safe investment, and training is a gated bet.

Retrieval on a frontier model, one of the largest general-purpose language models, is the safe default today. The evidence for limited-memory models is still research-stage: Wikipedia facts, models under 400 million parameters, and no dialog, voice or production use. A governed fact store pays off either way, so I would build it now and treat training as a bet to stage behind gates. Author's view.

Only the first of the three stages is a commitment; each of the others waits on the one before it. The staging is my recommendation.

The first stage starts now and carries no regret. Build the governed fact store.

Plans, policies, products, providers and fees become versioned rows with owners, access control and deletion. The store powers retrieval today and a limited-memory model later.

The second stage is a trial, once the store is ready. Trial a small model on one domain.

The trial starts from the open LMLM code (MIT license) on one de-identified domain, such as benefits or product FAQs. It goes further only if the model beats retrieval on factual precision, deletion, latency and total cost.

The third stage is scale, and only if the trial wins. Put it behind the responder.

The model then takes on more domains of static knowledge. Live balances, claims and appointment slots always stay on tools.

Compute is not the hard part. The paper trained each model in at most 8 H100-days of graphics-processor time, which is roughly 400 dollars to 500 dollars at 2 dollars to 2.69 dollars an hour for a rented H100. Author's arithmetic. The budget belongs with data engineering, store quality and evaluation.
