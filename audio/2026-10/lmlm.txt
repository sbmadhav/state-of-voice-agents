Section 9: What comes next.

Models that look facts up instead of memorizing them.

A Limited Memory Language Model (LMLM) is trained so that specific facts, such as a plan's coverage or a card's limit, live in an external database, not in the model's weights. During training the model is not rewarded for memorizing those values; it learns to issue a lookup and copy the answer. Language and reasoning stay in the model. Facts stay in a table people can inspect, correct and delete. It suits slow-changing knowledge such as benefits, policies and fees; live data such as balances, claims and appointment slots stays on tool and MCP calls.

Illustrative. The deletion behavior mirrors the paper's unlearning result: removing records from the store made the model forget them with no loss of general ability, where gradient-based unlearning degraded the model. Logs, transcripts, caches and backups still need their own deletion.

Why a governed fact store fits regulated conversations.

Facts become governed data. Each one is a row with access control, versioning and change review.

Deletion from the model is real. Removing rows forgets them without retraining. Copies in logs, transcripts and backups need their own retention rules.

Less sensitive data in weights. Researchers have extracted names, phone numbers and emails from model weights, even from data seen once.

Every answer has provenance. Each lookup is a logged call with the value it returned.

Room to shrink the model. In research, a 382 million limited-memory model came close to a 7 billion model on factual precision; details are in the technical appendix (T6).

My view: build the fact store now, and treat limited-memory training as a gated bet.

Retrieval on a frontier model is the safe default today, and the evidence for limited-memory models is still research-stage: Wikipedia facts, models under 400 million parameters, no dialog, voice or production use. A governed fact store pays off either way. Training is a bet to stage behind gates, not a commitment.
