T6. Limited Memory Language Models.

What the limited-memory research shows, and what it does not.

In its authors' tests, a 382 million model that looks facts up came close to a 7 billion model on factual accuracy. Nothing has been published yet on dialog, speech, models above about 400 million or enterprise data.

Section 9 introduced the Limited Memory Language Model (LMLM), which keeps facts in an external store and not in its weights. The research behind it is a paper by Zhao et al. "Pre-training Limited Memory Language Models with Internal and External Knowledge," at ICLR 2026. Its code and its 176-million-parameter and 382 million checkpoints are public under the MIT license. A contact center should care because facts held in a table can be inspected, corrected and deleted.

In training, the text is annotated with inline lookups: an entity, a relation and then the retrieved value. The training loss is zeroed on the tokens of the retrieved value. The model still trains on every other token, so it learns when and how to look up, not the value itself. The store holds 54.6 million (entity, relation, value) triples, extracted from Wikipedia by an 8 billion annotator trained on GPT-4o-labeled seeds. At inference a lookup token pauses generation, the query is matched by embedding similarity (with a rejection threshold of 0.6) and the value is inserted. The training budget was about 3 billion tokens, 8 epochs and at most 8 H100-days per model. The table shows what that bought.

With its store on, the 382 million LMLM scores close to the 7 billion LLaMA2 on FactScore and T-REx and well above it on PopQA; with the store off, it falls below the standard 382 million model. The scores, on three tests of factual accuracy, are those reported by Zhao et al. EM stands for exact match.

LLaMA2 standard, with 382 million parameters, scored 14.0 on FactScore, 52.0 on T-REx exact match and 22.7 on PopQA.

LLaMA2 LMLM, with 382 million parameters, scored 31.9 on FactScore, 58.1 on T-REx exact match and 50.8 on PopQA.

LLaMA2 LMLM, store off, with 382 million parameters, scored 12.8 on FactScore, 38.5 on T-REx exact match and has no score listed on PopQA.

LLaMA2, with 7 billion parameters, scored 34.0 on FactScore, 60.5 on T-REx exact match and 29.2 on PopQA.

LLaMA3.1, with 8 billion parameters, scored 40.3 on FactScore, 67.3 on T-REx exact match and 29.4 on PopQA.

The practical rival is retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), in which a standard model is handed retrieved text when it answers. In the same paper, a 355 million RAG baseline scored higher on T-REx than the same-size LMLM (75.8 versus 58.7) but lower on FactScore and PopQA, so retrieval-augmented frontier models remain the practical benchmark to beat.

A follow-up paper in July 2026, Co-LMLM, replaces the relational triples with vector keys and free-text values, so knowledge is no longer limited to entity attributes. A retrieval takes about 2.2 milliseconds, which fits inside a voice turn, against 28.6 milliseconds for a lookup that generates a question. Its authors also report that lookup timing is imperfect: forcing lookups added 5.9 points on SimpleQA.

How much of a model is fact storage? The research answers only indirectly, by showing how much smaller a model can be once it retrieves.

Retrieval models have matched models 18 to 50 times larger on knowledge-heavy tasks. Each bar is the parameter reduction at comparable performance reported for LMLM, RETRO and Atlas.

Parameter reduction at comparable performance.

LMLM, with 382 million parameters, is comparable to LLaMA2 with 7 billion, a reduction of 18 times. RETRO, with 7.5 billion, is comparable to GPT-3 with 175 billion, a reduction of 25 times. Atlas, with 11 billion, outscored a model with 540 billion on the NQ benchmark, a reduction of 50 times.

Models store about 2 bits of knowledge per parameter, falling toward 1 bit for rarely seen facts, and question-answering accuracy tracks how often a fact appears in training data. None of these studies measures the share of a frontier model's weights spent on facts, and reasoning, code and multilingual skill need parameters too. That gap is why several popular claims run ahead of the research. The table sets each beside a version the evidence supports.

None of five claims often made for limited-memory models holds as stated. The verdicts and the defensible versions are my reading of the papers cited. Author's view.

The claim is this: Every fact it shares is correct; it cannot hallucinate. I rate it overstated. It cuts hallucination sharply and makes each fact traceable, but errors from wrong lookups, missing rows and extraction remain.

The claim is this: It keeps non-repeating data out of the model. I rate it partly true. It offloads factual attributes by type, common and rare alike; rare facts are where models learn worst.

The claim is this: Fact storage is about 99 percent of model bloat. I rate it unsupported. Retrieval models match models 18 to 50 times larger on knowledge tasks, but the exact share is unmeasured.

The claim is this: It makes training cheap. I rate it partly true. It still trains on the full corpus and adds lookup and store-building cost; the saving is fewer parameters for a given factual accuracy, which lowers serving cost.

The claim is this: It keeps sensitive data out of weights. I rate it plausible but untested. It is designed to, and extraction attacks show why that matters, but no privacy-attack evaluation has been published.

Six things have to be true before a regulated contact center can rely on a limited-memory model. The list is my assessment. Author's view.

What has to be true for regulated contact centers.

Extraction has to be accurate. Turning policy text and records from customer-relationship (CRM) and electronic health record (EHR) systems into rows relies on LLM annotators, and their errors become confident wrong answers.

The store has to hold more than entity facts. Contact center knowledge is often procedural ("if the plan is X and the claim is Y"). Free-text values help, but that is unproven here.

Lookups have to be reliable. The model must know when to look up and must not fall back on its weights.

Live data still needs tools. Balances and appointments change by the minute, and an LMLM covers static knowledge, not transactions.

The total cost has to beat RAG. Pre-training a private small model, plus building and refreshing the store, must beat retrieval on a frontier API.

The evidence has to reach scale. There is nothing yet on dialog, speech, models above about 400 million or enterprise data. A 2026 position paper argues for this direction but reports no deployments.

The research shows a mechanism that works at small scale on encyclopedia facts. It does not yet show the same for the conversations, data and model sizes of a contact center, so for now it is something to test in stages, as section 9 proposes. The next section turns to where models and data run.
