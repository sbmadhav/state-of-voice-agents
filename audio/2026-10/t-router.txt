T5. System-1 routing.

For a fixed intent list, a small classifier is often the fastest and most accurate router.

On published figures, a fine-tuned classifier with 0.1 billion parameters labels banking questions faster and more accurately than a hosted decision model or a frontier model, one of the largest general-purpose language models. The figures come from different labs, so the comparison is directional.

Two of the failures in the previous section, the misroute and the decision flipped by extra context, happen in the router, the step that decides what a call is about before the agent speaks. The models built for that step are System-1 models, also called decision models: each returns a typed answer (a choice, a score, or a yes or no) with probabilities in one pass and never writes prose. Three families compete for the job: hosted decision APIs, open decision models and plain fine-tuned classifiers. A classifier does not win everywhere. In a study by Rodrigues and Vas, a fine-tuned RoBERTa classifier beat a small LLM (large language model) on the ATIS intent benchmark (95.9 percent versus 84.1 percent) and tied it on CLINC150, at roughly three orders of magnitude lower cost and latency. The LLM won on out-of-scope recall, the share of out-of-scope requests it catches (85.6 percent versus 58.1 percent), and on very noisy speech (92.5 percent versus 80.0 percent). A classifier trained on one app's intents scored 0 percent on a new app's, while a schema-prompted model served both at about 94 percent.

The two can be chained in a cascade, in which a cheap model answers first and passes on only the cases it is unsure of. In one public test, published by OpenRouter, on 3,080 banking questions across 77 intents, a frontier model scored 84.4 percent at about 2.42 dollars per 1,000 requests. Sending everything to a decision model first, and only the low-confidence cases (below 0.90) on to the frontier model, scored 84.0 percent at about 69 cents per 1,000. Vendor-reported.

The cascade cost about 72 percent less per 1,000 requests than the frontier model alone. It was scored on the same 3,080 examples used to measure accuracy, so the saving is an upper bound. Vendor-reported.

Cost per 1,000 requests.

The frontier model alone costs 2 dollars and 42 cents per 1,000 requests. With a decision model first, the cost is 69 cents.

Against this evidence, the thinker-responder thesis of section 5, a fast System-1 thinker that decides silently and a conversational LLM that speaks, mostly stands.

Two parts of the thesis hold, two need a qualification and one depends on a guardrail. The verdicts are my reading of the studies cited on each line. Author's view.

The thinker-responder thesis, checked.

This part holds. Routing a call to a sub-agent or toolset is closed-set classification, a choice from a fixed list. Small decision models do it in roughly 20 to 250 milliseconds for fractions of a cent, versus about 2 seconds for a frontier LLM.

This part holds. Narrowing tools first helps. In one study, retrieving the right tools before selection lifted selection accuracy from 13.6 percent to 43.1 percent and cut prompt tokens by more than half; Anthropic reports similar gains once agents have 10 or more tools.

This part needs a qualification. For a fixed intent list, a plain fine-tuned classifier is often both the fastest and the most accurate router. Hosted decision APIs earn their place when intents change often or labeled data is scarce.

This part needs a qualification. Decision models are not uniformly "great at decisions." Published accuracy for the same model on the same benchmark varies by more than 10 points across labs, and benign-looking context flipped about 61 percent of correct decisions in one study.

This part needs a guardrail. Fraud, clinical concern, complaints and vulnerable-customer signals escalate by rule, never by a threshold. Multi-intent calls ("overcharged again and nobody calls back") need multi-label output, which can return more than one intent.

The same narrowing applies to tools, which agents reach through Model Context Protocol (MCP) servers. Anthropic reports that 58 tools across 5 servers consume about 55K tokens before a conversation starts, and that tool search lifted accuracy from 49 percent to 74 percent on one model. On one benchmark, MCP-Zero selected from about 2,800 tools across 308 servers with 98 percent fewer tokens. Retrieval recall then becomes the new bottleneck: with 4,027 tools, the right tool reached the top results only 56 percent to 64 percent of the time. Taken together, the evidence supports a router design in ten parts.

The recommended router has ten parts, from the classifier to the audit log and the compliance terms. The design is my recommendation. Author's view.

Recommended router design.

First, tier 0 is a fine-tuned encoder or a self-hosted decision model with multi-label output (one sigmoid per intent), so that "billing and complaint" returns both. In bilingual markets it should start from a multilingual encoder such as XLM-R and be tested on turns that mix languages, because English-only encoders misroute them.

Second, the stop control lets the thinker flush and mute the audio output immediately when it flags risk while the agent is already speaking, and then hand off.

Third, calibration uses temperature scaling, a one-parameter rescaling of the model's probabilities, on the team's own data. Per-intent thresholds are set on a held-out set, never on the test set.

Fourth, the gate sends a turn that is below threshold, out of scope or carrying two high-stakes intents to a frontier model with the full schema, or to a person.

Fifth, hard rules always escalate fraud, clinical concern, complaints and vulnerable-customer signals.

Sixth, narrow tools mean that each sub-agent exposes only its own MCP servers, with a fallback to broader tools when retrieval scores are low.

Seventh, injection hygiene means that the router classifies the caller's turn only, with no retrieved or third-party text in context.

Eighth, state lives in one call-state object per call, held by the orchestrator. The thinker runs on every finished turn and writes typed fields (intent, risk, speaker, emotion, confidence). The responder owns the conversation, slot filling and clarifying questions ("does Dr. Smith work Fridays?"), and reads the thinker's fields before it speaks. A new intent, topic shift or correction triggers a re-route; slots already filled carry over.

Ninth, the audit log records the input hash, label, probabilities, threshold, model version and final disposition, and evaluations are re-run on every model change.

Tenth, compliance depends on hosting: a hosted decision API needs a BAA or equivalent for protected data, and a self-hosted encoder avoids the question.

What a team should take from the comparison is a division of labor: a classifier for stable, high-volume intents, a decision API or an LLM for new, rare or out-of-scope ones, and rules, not thresholds, for fraud, clinical concern and complaints.
