T5. System-1 routing.

For a fixed intent list, a small classifier is often the fastest and most accurate router.

On published figures, a fine-tuned classifier with 0.1 billion parameters labels banking questions faster and more accurately than a hosted decision model or a frontier model, one of the largest general-purpose language models. The figures come from different labs, so the comparison is directional.

The previous section traced two failures to the router, the step that decides what a call is about before the agent speaks. A System-1 model returns a typed answer (a choice, a score, or a yes or no) with probabilities in one pass and never writes prose. Three families compete for the routing job: hosted decision APIs, open decision models and plain fine-tuned classifiers. The table reports what each maker, model card or outside test has published.

The eight options run from hosted APIs priced per token to open models that a team hosts itself, and four have a latency measured in milliseconds.

OpenAI Decisions API. It is closed and hosted, in beta since October 6, 2026. It returns a predicate (a yes or no), a choice or a score, with probabilities. OpenAI says it is about 10 times faster than Responses. Vendor-reported. It costs 10 cents per million input tokens, and output is free. It takes text and images, and is offered under HIPAA, the US health privacy law, to eligible customers with a business associate agreement (BAA), the contract that law requires of vendors that handle patient data.

TypeSafe Jev. It is closed and hosted. It returns a Choice, a Score or a Noul (a 0 to 1 "is this true?" answer), with probabilities. One test measured a median of 175 milliseconds and a 95th percentile of 270 milliseconds. It costs 4.2 cents per million input tokens, or about 11 cents per 1,000 calls. It scored 81.0 percent on Banking77 against 84.4 percent for a frontier model, and it has no public BAA.

Laya (Convai). It is open under Apache-2.0 and comes in two sizes, 421 million and 322 million multilingual. It has the same typed interface. It answers in 33 to 40 milliseconds on a T4 GPU and can run on a CPU. It is self-hosted. Its routed base checkpoint scored 42.5 percent on Banking77. It reached 76.6 percent on its own typed-decisions benchmark after fine-tuning on that benchmark's training split.

Arch-Router-1.5 billion (Katanemo). It has open weights. It returns a route name. It answers in 51 milliseconds on an L40S GPU. It is self-hosted. Katanemo reports 93.2 percent routing accuracy, against 92.8 percent for a frontier model.

Fine-tuned ModernBERT-base. It is an open encoder with 0.1 billion parameters. It returns an intent label. It answers in 22.5 milliseconds on a T4 GPU. It is self-hosted. Its model card reports 93.5 percent on Banking77.

Semantic-router (Aurelio). It is open under the MIT license. It returns a route, or none. It takes one embedding call. It costs one embedding call. It returns "none" below a threshold, which suits out-of-scope requests.

Sierra Curie. It is closed, inside the Sierra platform. It orchestrates across what Sierra calls its "constellation" of models. Sierra publishes no latency figure. It is in platform pricing. Sierra announced it in October 2026 and also runs Fleming to detect bot and agent callers. Vendor-reported.

RouteLLM. It is open. It picks a strong or a weak model. It has no latency or cost entry. Its authors report that it cuts cost "over 2 times" without quality loss. It routes between models, not intents.

Two charts compare the options on accuracy and on speed. The accuracy test is Banking77, a benchmark of banking questions sorted into 77 intents, on which Jev was scored zero-shot, with no training on the task.

A small fine-tuned classifier leads both charts, with 93.5 percent on Banking77 and 22.5 milliseconds to route one decision. Different labs measured these on different setups and hardware, so both charts are directional.

On Banking77, a fine-tuned ModernBERT with 0.1 billion parameters scores 93.5 percent and a bge-small embedding model with logistic regression 93.3. A frontier model, Opus 5, scores 84.4, Jev used zero-shot 81.0 and the routed Laya base model 42.5.

Jev's own score ranges from 76.3 percent to 87.0 percent depending on who measured it.

To route one decision, a fine-tuned encoder takes 22.5 milliseconds, the open Laya model 36, Arch-Router 51, and hosted Jev 175 at the median and 270 at the 95th percentile. A frontier model takes 2.27 seconds at the median, well past the 800-millisecond budget for a whole turn.

The frontier figure is a large model classifying Banking77 intents.

A classifier does not win everywhere, and a study by Rodrigues and Vas shows where. A fine-tuned RoBERTa classifier beat a small LLM on the ATIS intent benchmark (95.9 percent versus 84.1 percent) and tied it on CLINC150, at roughly three orders of magnitude lower cost and latency. The LLM won on out-of-scope recall, the share of out-of-scope requests it catches (85.6 percent versus 58.1 percent), and on very noisy speech (92.5 percent versus 80.0 percent). A classifier trained on one app's intents scored 0 percent on a new app's, while a schema-prompted model served both at about 94 percent.

The two can be chained in a cascade, in which a cheap model answers first and passes on only the cases it is unsure of. In one public test, published by OpenRouter, on 3,080 banking questions across 77 intents, a frontier model scored 84.4 percent at about 2.42 dollars per 1,000 requests. Sending everything to a decision model first, and only the low-confidence cases (below 0.90) on to the frontier model, scored 84.0 percent at about 69 cents per 1,000.

The cascade cost about 72 percent less per 1,000 requests than the frontier model alone. It was scored on the same 3,080 examples used to measure accuracy, so the saving is an upper bound. Vendor-reported.

Section 5 argued for a thinker-responder design, in which a fast System-1 thinker decides silently and a conversational LLM speaks. Checked against the evidence, the thesis divides into five parts.

Two parts of the thesis hold, two need a qualification and one needs a guardrail. The verdicts are my reading of the studies cited on each line.

This part holds. Routing a call to a sub-agent or toolset is closed-set classification, a choice from a fixed list. Small decision models do it in roughly 20 to 250 milliseconds for fractions of a cent, versus about 2 seconds for a frontier LLM.

This part holds. Narrowing tools first helps. In one study, retrieving the right tools before selection lifted selection accuracy from 13.6 percent to 43.1 percent and cut prompt tokens by more than half. Anthropic reports similar gains once agents have 10 or more tools.

This part needs a qualification. For a fixed intent list, a plain fine-tuned classifier is often both the fastest and the most accurate router. Hosted decision APIs earn their place when intents change often or labeled data is scarce.

This part needs a qualification. Decision models are not uniformly "great at decisions." Published accuracy for the same model on the same benchmark varies by more than 10 points across labs, and benign-looking context flipped about 61 percent of correct decisions in one study.

This part needs a guardrail. Fraud, clinical concern, complaints and vulnerable-customer signals escalate by rule, never by a threshold. Multi-intent calls ("overcharged again and nobody calls back") need multi-label output.

Routing also chooses tools, which an agent reaches through Model Context Protocol (MCP) servers. Anthropic reports that 58 tools across 5 servers consume about 55K tokens before a conversation starts, and that tool search lifted accuracy from 49 percent to 74 percent on one model. On one benchmark, MCP-Zero selected from about 2,800 tools across 308 servers with 98 percent fewer tokens. Retrieval recall then becomes the new bottleneck: with 4,027 tools, the right tool reached the top results only 56 percent to 64 percent of the time. The router design I recommend builds on these findings.

The design has ten parts, from the classifier to the audit log and the compliance terms. Author's view.

First, tier 0 is a fine-tuned encoder or a self-hosted decision model with multi-label output (one sigmoid per intent), so that "billing and complaint" returns both. In bilingual markets it should start from a multilingual encoder such as XLM-R and be tested on turns that mix languages, because English-only encoders misroute them.

Second, the stop control lets the thinker flush and mute the audio output immediately when it flags risk while the agent is already speaking, and then hand off.

Third, calibration uses temperature scaling, a one-parameter rescaling of the model's probabilities, on the team's own data. Per-intent thresholds are set on a held-out set, never on the test set.

Fourth, the gate sends a turn that is below threshold, out of scope or carrying two high-stakes intents to a frontier model with the full schema, or to a person.

Fifth, hard rules always escalate fraud, clinical concern, complaints and vulnerable-customer signals.

Sixth, narrow tools mean that each sub-agent exposes only its own MCP servers, with a fallback to broader tools when retrieval scores are low.

Seventh, injection hygiene means that the router classifies the caller's turn only, with no retrieved or third-party text in context.

Eighth, state lives in one call-state object per call, held by the orchestrator. The thinker runs on every finished turn and writes typed fields (intent, risk, speaker, emotion, confidence). The responder owns the conversation, slot filling and clarifying questions ("does Dr. Smith work Fridays?"), and reads the thinker's fields before it speaks. A new intent, topic shift or correction triggers a re-route; slots already filled carry over.

Ninth, the audit log records the input hash, label, probabilities, threshold, model version and final disposition, and evaluations are re-run on every model change.

Tenth, compliance depends on hosting: a hosted decision API needs a BAA or equivalent for protected data, and a self-hosted encoder avoids the question.

The comparison points to a division of labor: a classifier for stable, high-volume intents, and a decision API or an LLM for new, rare or out-of-scope ones. T6 turns from how an agent decides to where it keeps its facts.
