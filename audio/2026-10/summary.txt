Executive summary. About ten minutes.

The argument in nine points.

The report's argument runs in nine steps, from what the evidence shows to what to do in the next 90 days. Each point links to the section that holds its evidence.

Point 1.

Agents can take the routine calls, but not yet the same way every time.

In one public benchmark, the best platform completed over 90 percent of scenarios on a single try but passed only 75.6 percent when each scenario had to succeed three times in a row. The gap is consistency, not capability. Health systems report large gains, such as waits cut from 6.2 to 2.4 minutes and 44 percent of repetitive calls automated, but every published result is administrative and self-reported.

Point 2.

Evaluation is the asset that lasts.

Models change every month: the top score on one voice benchmark rose from 30 percent to 67 percent in eight months. What a team keeps is its golden test set, hundreds of real, de-identified call scenarios with known right answers, including noisy lines and upset callers, run three times on every change. A drop in score blocks the release, the way failing tests block code. The set is worth building before choosing a vendor, because it is how a team compares vendors, gates releases and holds contracts to account.

Point 3.

An agent should think silently, then respond.

People size up a question before they answer it, and a voice agent should too. A small, fast "System 1" model answers typed questions in tens of milliseconds, each with a confidence score. The questions include which team owns the call, whether there is fraud or clinical risk, whether the caller has finished and whether it is even the caller speaking. The model never writes prose. A conversational LLM then responds, with the right data already fetched.

The split is faster and cheaper. In one test, routing first kept accuracy at about 84 percent while cutting cost from 2.42 dollars to about 69 cents per 1,000 requests. It is also safer, because every decision is a logged record, and fraud, clinical and complaint signals always go to a person by rule. The fast model works beside any voice stack, and it can cut the audio mid-sentence if it spots risk.

Point 4.

The agent has to hear the right caller.

Real calls come from cars, kitchens and waiting rooms. In one vendor test, background speech pushed transcription errors to between 22 percent and 31 percent, and isolating the main speaker cut them by up to 43 percent. Diarization separates the voices, so the agent can lock onto the caller and check identity again if a different voice takes over before a sensitive step. Tests therefore need noise and side conversations, not clean demo audio.

Point 5.

Emotion and persona are worth piloting but not yet proven.

Detecting frustration in real time lets the agent slow down or hand off to a person, and scoring sentiment on every call replaces quality reviews that sample a few percent. A persona is the brand's voice. Callers can be offered a choice and then hear their preferred voice on every call. The evidence is still mostly vendor case studies, and the EU requires telling people when emotion recognition is used, so both belong behind the golden test set as experiments.

Point 6.

Governed memory comes first, and limited-memory models later.

Plans, policies, fees and provider details belong in a governed fact store: versioned rows with owners, access control and deletion. It powers retrieval today. Live data such as balances and appointment slots always comes from the organization's own systems through tools.

Limited-memory models go further, because they are trained to look facts up in that store instead of memorizing them. In research, a 382 million-parameter model approached a 7 billion model on factual precision, and deleting a row made the model forget the fact. That research is early: Wikipedia facts, small models, no voice or production use. The sensible order is therefore to trial it on one domain once the store is ready and to scale only if it beats retrieval.

Point 7.

A voice is no longer proof of identity.

Over half of fraud attempts at health care contact centers now involve AI-generated elements such as synthetic voice, a detection vendor reports. The check should therefore match the risk: passive signals for status checks, a one-time code for account changes, and a voiceprint plus liveness detection for irreversible actions, with a person always available as the fallback. Liveness detection tests that the speaker is a live person, not a recording or a clone. Card numbers should never reach the agent at all. For outbound calls, consent belongs to the current subscriber, so lists need checking against the Reassigned Numbers Database of the Federal Communications Commission (FCC).

Point 8.

The money is in resolution, not token price.

Savings come from calls the agent truly resolves, meaning no repeat contact on any channel within 48 hours. The calls left for people are the harder ones, so honest models show smaller savings than vendor pitches. With this report's default assumptions, the agent breaks even at about 40 percent resolution, and each extra point of resolution is worth far more than cheaper tokens. That argues for a contract priced per resolved call, with rights to export logs and evaluation data.

Point 9.

The next 90 days should go to a narrow pilot with fixed gates.

The pilot should take two or three inbound administrative call types in one market, and it starts with the golden test set, 200 or more scenarios. The go-live bar rises with risk: about 85 percent repeat reliability for informational calls, 95 percent for anything that writes data or moves money, and zero critical failures. A call the agent hands off goes to a person with full context. At day 90 the pilot is judged on cost per resolved call, abandonment and zero wrong-account actions. Whatever the outcome, the test set, routing logs and recordings are worth keeping, because they are the most valuable thing the pilot produces.
