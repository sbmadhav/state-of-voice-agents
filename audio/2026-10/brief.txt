State of conversational voice agents. Health care first, with notes for financial services.

Voice agents at the front door: ready for routine calls, not for unsupervised high-risk ones.

What separates the teams that succeed with voice agents is not which model they buy. It is how they test, how the agent decides, what it remembers, who it is listening to and how it proves who the caller is.

By Madhav Srinivasan. Published October 12, 2026.

The text was edited for readability on November 15, 2026. The findings and figures are as first published. The latest update is the November 2026 edition.

Tampa General reports that within two weeks of going live with a voice agent, the average wait for its callers fell from 6.2 minutes to 2.4. The share who hung up before being answered, known as call abandonment, fell from 34 percent to 14.9 percent. Self-reported. Voice agents are now good enough to take routine calls in regulated contact centers: scheduling, billing questions, balance and status checks. They are not yet consistent enough to run unsupervised on clinical or high-risk financial conversations. In one vendor-run benchmark of seven platforms, the leading one still failed about one test scenario in four when each of 82 was run three times.

The one-minute read.

This review rests on public sources; I did not run my own tests. The top three platforms in that benchmark are within its margin of error, and every published deployment result is administrative and self-reported. Demos overstate readiness, cloned voices defeat voice-only identity checks, and scores move so fast that a bet on one model goes stale in a quarter.

A team should build a golden test set from its own calls and gate every change on it. A pilot should stay narrow and be judged on cost per resolved call.

Four figures frame the report. All four are vendor-reported or self-reported.

Fall in call abandonment: 56 percent. Tampa General reports the drop within two weeks of going live with its voice agent.

Best repeat reliability: 75.6 percent. In Cekura's benchmark, which a testing vendor runs, the leading platform passed this share of 82 scenarios on all three tries (about 65 percent to 84 percent at 95 percent confidence).

Can quantify AI's impact: 5 percent. Only this share of customer experience, IT and operations leaders can quantify AI's impact on business outcomes, a Talkdesk and NewtonX report says, though 98 percent have deployed it in the customer journey. Vendor-reported.

Fraud attempts involving AI: Over half. Pindrop, a deepfake-detection vendor, reports that this share of fraud attempts at health care contact centers now involves AI-generated elements such as synthetic voice.

Thesis 1: Reliability, not capability, is the gap.

In one benchmark, single-run scores beat repeat reliability by 18 to 38 points on six of seven platforms, so a team should test repeatability on its own calls.

Thesis 2: An agent should decide silently, then speak.

People do not narrate every thought before they answer. A small, fast model, called System 1 after the term for quick, intuitive judgment, decides silently, and a conversational large language model (LLM) speaks.

Thesis 3: Governed memory pays off now; the models built on it are still research.

A governed fact store, in which every fact has an owner and a version, pays off today through retrieval, the current way of fetching facts for a model. Limited-memory models, which look facts up in the store instead of memorizing them, are promising but still research, so they should be trialed against retrieval first.

Four evidence labels are used throughout this report: Independent. Vendor or self-reported. Unverified. Author's view, arithmetic or illustration. Charts and tools that respond to input are marked interactive.

How to read this report: method, limits and disclosure.

This is a review of public sources up to the research cutoff. I did not run my own tests, and every figure links to its source.

A platform or model appears when it publishes benchmark results or deployment outcomes, or runs at large health systems or banks. Sierra, an enterprise agent platform, is included in the market analysis wherever evidence exists, because of its health care footprint. It was not part of the seven-platform reliability test, and I note that gap instead of filling it in.

I weigh the evidence in a fixed order: peer-reviewed and independent tests first, then vendor benchmarks with a published method, then customer-reported outcomes, then vendor claims. The label on each figure says which it is.

The interactive tools are illustrative. The router, latency, economics and limited-memory demos are models for exploring trade-offs, not measurements of a product.

The recommendations are proposals. A team should adapt the thresholds, pilot gates and suggested paths to its own risk, call mix and baseline.
