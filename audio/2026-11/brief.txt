State of conversational voice agents. Monthly update. Financial services focus, with health care notes.

Banks and credit unions reach the front door.

This month's update reviews bank and credit union results for the first time, reports a new leader on the reliability benchmark and corrects October's advice on voice identity checks. The new risk it reports is an ordinary one: a television playing in the room broke most voice agents in one test.

By Madhav Srinivasan. Published November 10, 2026. Updated November 15, 2026.

This update builds on the October 2026 baseline and covers only what changed since then, or is reviewed for the first time.

Banks and credit unions are putting voice agents on the same kind of call that health systems chose first: the routine one. Their published results cover calls such as balances, payments and branch hours, and every performance figure comes from the institution or its vendor. The one dated share of calls is also the smallest. Southern Cross Credit Union's voice agent takes 7 percent of all calls (reported October 27), against the 37 percent to 90 percent claimed in undated vendor case studies. Vendor-reported.

The new risk comes from outside banking. Veris called 14 voice systems as a simulated utility-billing customer. With a television playing in the room, task completion fell from 38.7 percent on clean calls to 8.6 percent, and most calls carried words the caller never said. Vendor-reported. The calls were simulated and vendors' noise options were off, so the levels are directional, but in banking those inserted words are the risk, in a security answer or a payment amount. Author's view.

Nine findings moved in all. Among the others, a new entrant topped the benchmark of repeat reliability (pass three), the share of test scenarios an agent passes on all three tries. The Federal Communications Commission (FCC) also changed the opt-out rules under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA).

Four figures summarize the month. The first three are vendor-reported, and the opt-out date comes from independent sources.

Best repeat reliability: 87.8 percent. October's best was 75.6 percent, among 7 platforms. PolyAI, a new entrant, leads ten configurations, but its range (about 79 percent to 93 percent) overlaps the runner-up's.

Share of all calls, dated bank result: 7 percent. October did not review bank results. Southern Cross Credit Union's voice agent takes 7 percent of all calls, mostly for balances. Undated vendor case studies claim 37 percent to 90 percent.

Task completion, TV on: 8.6 percent. The same test scored 38.7 percent on clean calls. Veris measured this on simulated calls to 14 voice systems.

New FCC opt-out rules take effect: November 25. October did not review these rules. FCC order 26-67 lets the organization placing a call designate one or more approved opt-out methods, which it must disclose on the call. Opt-outs can be limited to a message category, and the fraud-alert exemption is wider.

Unchanged since October.

October's three theses hold: reliability is the gap, an agent should think silently, then respond, and a team should invest in governed memory. The economics model, the 90-day pilot and the router comparison stand too. Everything else is indexed at the end, with a link to its October section.

How to read this update: method, labels and disclosure.

Findings published between October 12 and November 14, 2026 carry a "new" tag. Older material appears only where October did not cover it, as with most of the financial services evidence. Where a figure changed, the October value is shown next to it.

Four labels say where a claim comes from: independent, vendor-reported, company-reported and author's view. Every figure links to its source.

The method is a review of public sources up to the research cutoff. I did not run my own tests.

Besides repeat reliability, three terms recur. Word error rate is the share of words transcribed wrongly. Full-duplex models listen and talk at the same time. A System-1 model makes fast typed decisions (route, risk, done talking) without writing prose.
