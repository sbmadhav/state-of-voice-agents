How to read this update: method, labels and disclosure
Findings published between October 12 and November 14, 2026 carry a NEW tag. Older material appears only where October did not cover it, as with most of the financial services evidence. Where a figure changed, the October value is shown next to it.

3 · NEW FAILURE MODE

A second voice in the room breaks most voice agents

October listed background chatter as one failure among many (T4). A test on simulated calls this month puts it first.

The noise that hurts most is not traffic or wind. It is another voice. The evidence comes from a Veris test NEW that called 14 voice systems as a simulated utility-billing customer. It ran 100 tasks three times each (1,906 calls scored) and mixed street, café and TV audio into the calls at equal loudness.

With the TV on, task completion fell from 38.7% (372 of 961 calls) to 8.6% (81 of 945). Nine of the 14 systems fell below 5%, and only GPT-Live 1 (36.1%) and Grok Voice (33.8%) stayed above 20%. Caller verification fell from 81.3% to 52.5%, and 90% of TV calls carried 10 or more words the caller never said, against 2% of clean calls.[3] vendor-reported Vendors' noise options were left off and the calls were simulated, so the levels are directional.

I recommend four controls to keep a second voice from steering the agent. author's view
Isolate the caller's voice before speech-to-text and turn detection.
Let a voice interrupt the agent only when it matches the caller.
Read back identity details, amounts and account numbers before any write, and re-verify if a different voice answers a security question.
Add TV, café and second-speaker audio to the golden test set, the team's own call scenarios with known right answers, and block releases on it.

The last of those controls outlasts any single result, as the next section shows: the reliability benchmark changed leaders this month.

6 · FRAUD AND IDENTITY

Correction: a voice is a risk signal, not a way to approve a payment

This section corrects the October edition. Its risk tiers (T8) put voiceprint plus liveness at the top, which missed guidance already in place.

The guidance is New York's. In October 2024 the state's financial regulator advised against using voice as an authentication factor, preferring liveness checks or phishing-resistant methods.[13] independent

New research reinforces the change. RealComm NEW, a benchmark of deepfake detection over real communication channels, found that detectors degrade substantially after the audio crosses a real phone line. On call audio, one detector's equal error rate (where missed fakes equal false alarms) was 37.9%, and 22.4% after retraining on call audio.[14] independent

The fraud figures show what is at stake. Pindrop's 2025 report counted a rise in synthetic-voice attacks of 149% at banks and 475% at insurers in 2024, across 1.2 billion calls analyzed.[53] vendor-reported One credit union's deepfake detection is reported to have avoided $2.57M of fraud exposure in about 13 months.[54] vendor-reported US consumers reported $3.5B lost to impostor scams in 2025, and bank impersonators accounted for nearly $1B, more than government impersonators.[55] independent Generative AI could push US fraud losses from $12.3B in 2023 to $40B by 2027, a projection by Deloitte.[56] independent

The top tier is therefore revised. Payments, payee changes and limit increases should go through an app push or another phishing-resistant check. Voiceprint and liveness stay as risk signals that can block a transaction, never as the only factor that approves one. author's view

7 · MODELS, PLATFORMS AND ROUTING

OpenAI's decision API left beta, and two new models are too slow for live voice

The releases that change a recommendation come first, and a short list of everything else follows. The architecture and routing analysis is in October, T1 and T5.

OpenAI's Decisions API NEW was in public beta in October and became generally available on October 22. It is a System-1 service, one that makes fast typed decisions (route, risk, done talking) without writing prose. It runs on GPT-6 Luna, takes up to 400 questions in a request and costs $0.10 per million input tokens. GPT-Live, OpenAI's voice model, sends turns to it while the caller is still speaking, and Sierra, a customer, reported a 95th-percentile latency under 280 ms in launch coverage.[15][57] vendor-reported

Speed cuts the other way for two new language models. Regal's benchmark for voice agents NEW ran on October 15. GPT-6.1 Sol (1,711 ms) and Gemini 3.8 Flash (2,516 ms) missed the bar of 1,300 ms that Regal sets for live voice, while GPT-6 Sol led at 786 of 1,000.[58] vendor-reported

A licensed provider must exercise independent judgment when AI output informs care, AI may not perform reserved clinical functions, and health care chatbot operators are treated as providers under the state's medical privacy law. STATE OF CONVERSATIONAL VOICE AGENTS · HEALTH CARE FIRST, WITH NOTES FOR FINANCIAL SERVICES

Voice agents at the front door: ready for routine calls, not for unsupervised high-risk ones

What separates the teams that succeed with voice agents is not which model they buy. It is how they test, how the agent decides, what it remembers, who it is listening to and how it proves who the caller is.

By Madhav Srinivasan. Published October 12, 2026.

The text was edited for readability on November 15, 2026. The findings and figures are as first published. The latest update is the November 2026 edition.

Tampa General reports that within two weeks of going live with a voice agent, the average wait for its callers fell from 6.2 minutes to 2.4. The share who hung up before being answered, known as call abandonment, fell from 34% to 14.9%.[1] self-reported Voice agents are now good enough to take routine calls in regulated contact centers: scheduling, billing questions, balance and status checks. They are not yet consistent enough to run unsupervised on clinical or high-risk financial conversations. In one vendor-run benchmark of seven platforms, the leading one still failed about one test scenario in four when each of 82 was run three times.#

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY · ABOUT TEN MINUTES

The argument in nine points

The report's argument runs in nine steps, from what the evidence shows to what to do in the next 90 days. Each point links to the section that holds its evidence.

1
Agents can take the routine calls, but not yet the same way every time

In one public benchmark, the best platform completed over 90% of scenarios on a single try but passed only 75.6% when each scenario had to succeed three times in a row.[2] The gap is consistency, not capability. Health systems report large gains, such as waits cut from 6.2 to 2.4 minutes and 44% of repetitive calls automated, but every published result is administrative and self-reported.[5][6]

Reliability and results
2
Evaluation is the asset that lasts

Models change every month: the top score on one voice benchmark rose from 30% to 67% in eight months.[7] What a team keeps is its golden test set, hundreds of real, de-identified call scenarios with known right answers, including noisy lines and upset callers, run three times on every change. A drop in score blocks the release, the way failing tests block code. The set is worth building before choosing a vendor, because it is how a team compares vendors, gates releases and holds contracts to account.

The golden test set
3
An agent should think silently, then respond

People size up a question before they answer it, and a voice agent should too. A small, fast "System 1" model answers typed questions in tens of milliseconds, each with a confidence score. The questions include which team owns the call, whether there is fraud or clinical risk, whether the caller has finished and whether it is even the caller speaking. The model never writes prose. A conversational LLM then responds, with the right data already fetched.

The split is faster and cheaper. In one test, routing first kept accuracy at about 84% while cutting cost from $2.42 to about $0.69 per 1,000 requests.[8] It is also safer, because every decision is a logged record, and fraud, clinical and complaint signals always go to a person by rule. The fast model works beside any voice stack, and it can cut the audio mid-sentence if it spots risk.

The thinker-responder model
4
The agent has to hear the right caller

Real calls come from cars, kitchens and waiting rooms. In one vendor test, background speech pushed transcription errors to between 22% and 31%, and isolating the main speaker cut them by up to 43%.[9] Diarization separates the voices, so the agent can lock onto the caller and check identity again if a different voice takes over before a sensitive step. Tests therefore need noise and side conversations, not clean demo audio.

Noise and diarization
5
Emotion and persona are worth piloting but not yet proven

Detecting frustration in real time lets the agent slow down or hand off to a person, and scoring sentiment on every call replaces quality reviews that sample a few percent. A persona is the brand's voice. Callers can be offered a choice and then hear their preferred voice on every call. The evidence is still mostly vendor case studies, and the EU requires telling people when emotion recognition is used,[10] so both belong behind the golden test set as experiments.

Emotion and persona
6
Governed memory comes first, and limited-memory models later

Plans, policies, fees and provider details belong in a governed fact store: versioned rows with owners, access control and deletion. It powers retrieval today. Live data such as balances and appointment slots always comes from the organization's own systems through tools.

Limited-memory models go further, because they are trained to look facts up in that store instead of memorizing them. In research, a 382M-parameter model approached a 7B model on factual precision, and deleting a row made the model forget the fact.[11] That research is early: Wikipedia facts, small models, no voice or production use. The sensible order is therefore to trial it on one domain once the store is ready and to scale only if it beats retrieval.

Governed memory
7
A voice is no longer proof of identity

Over half of fraud attempts at health care contact centers now involve AI-generated elements such as synthetic voice, a detection vendor reports.[4] The check should therefore match the risk: passive signals for status checks, a one-time code for account changes, and a voiceprint plus liveness detection for irreversible actions, with a person always available as the fallback. Liveness detection tests that the speaker is a live person, not a recording or a clone. Card numbers should never reach the agent at all. For outbound calls, consent belongs to the current subscriber, so lists need checking against the Reassigned Numbers Database of the Federal Communications Commission (FCC).[12]

Trust and identity
8
The money is in resolution, not token price

Savings come from calls the agent truly resolves, meaning no repeat contact on any channel within 48 hours. The calls left for people are the harder ones, so honest models show smaller savings than vendor pitches. With this report's default assumptions, the agent breaks even at about 40% resolution, and each extra point of resolution is worth far more than cheaper tokens. That argues for a contract priced per resolved call, with rights to export logs and evaluation data.

The economics model
9
The next 90 days should go to a narrow pilot with fixed gates

The pilot should take two or three inbound administrative call types in one market, and it starts with the golden test set, 200 or more scenarios. The go-live bar rises with risk: about 85% repeat reliability for informational calls, 95% for anything that writes data or moves money, and zero critical failures. A call the agent hands off goes to a person with full context. At day 90 the pilot is judged on cost per resolved call, abandonment and zero wrong-account actions. Whatever the outcome, the test set, routing logs and recordings are worth keeping, because they are the most valuable thing the pilot produces.

The pilot playbook

5 · THINK SILENTLY, THEN RESPOND

A voice agent should decide silently before it speaks

People size up a question before they answer, and they do not say every thought aloud. A voice agent can work the same way if a fast model decides silently and a conversational model speaks.

This report calls the design thinker-responder. The thinker is a fast System 1 model: a small model that returns typed answers, each with a probability, and never writes prose. OpenAI's Decisions API, the open-source Laya and TypeSafe's Jev are examples.[22][20][19] The responder is a conversational LLM that turns those decisions into a natural reply.

The first demo plays one caller turn, "I got charged twice for my visit last month," both ways. The thing to notice is what the caller hears before the first useful word.

On the same caller turn, the thinker-responder agent answers directly, and a single model that reasons aloud keeps the caller waiting about 2 seconds for the first useful word. The exchange is illustrative, not a recording of a product. illustrative

The caller hears one direct answer. The decisions are logged as structured data, not spoken.

On each caller turn the thinker settles seven things.

Intent is the sub-agent that owns the turn, such as billing, scheduling or complaints.
Risk covers the fraud, clinical, vulnerable-caller and complaint flags that force a handoff.
Tools are the servers to expose over the Model Context Protocol (MCP), a standard for connecting a model to outside systems, and the facts to fetch before speaking.
Turn state says whether the caller is done, is correcting themselves or is just saying "mm-hmm".
Speaker says whether this is the caller or someone else in the room.
Emotion registers frustration or confusion that should change the tone, slow things down or prompt a handoff.
Confidence is a probability on each of the others, so that a low-confidence turn is handed off instead of guessed at.

The split pays off in three ways. It is faster, because the decisions take tens to hundreds of milliseconds and the responder starts with its data already fetched. It is cheaper, because the expensive model writes only the reply, not the routing. And it is safer, because the "thought" is a structured record, not prose, so it can be logged, audited and gated by rules, and the responder cannot narrate a tool result it never received.[23]

OpenAI and Sierra both put the fast model on the talking side and a slower reasoner behind it.[26][14] This design differs deliberately: it puts the fast model on the deciding side. The two combine into a fast talker, a fast silent decider and a slower reasoner for when one is needed.

The thinker works with any voice stack, because it does not need a speech-to-text pipeline. Beside a speech-to-speech model, or a full-duplex one that listens and speaks at the same time, it reads the transcript stream and decides which tools and policies apply, so the voice model keeps its natural turn-taking. A full-duplex model starts speaking within a few hundred milliseconds, so the thinker also needs a stop control. When it flags risk in the middle of a turn, it cuts the audio output at once, before protected data or advice reaches the caller.

The thinker has a limit: it handles bounded choices. Open-ended reasoning still needs a deeper planner that the responder can call.

6 · WHAT SEPARATES A GOOD AGENT

Two capabilities outlast any model, and two are still experiments

Two capabilities decide whether an agent holds up on real calls, and they carry over from one model or vendor to the next. Two more are promising but early.

Models change every month. The two foundations are a golden test set with an evaluation framework around it, and audio handling that picks the caller's voice out of a noisy room. The two experiments, reading the caller's emotion and tuning the agent's persona, are worth piloting behind the golden test set.

The two foundations come with targets a team can test against, and the two experiments come with cautions. Vendor figures are labeled, and the estimate of test runs is my arithmetic.
FOUNDATION
A golden test set gates every change

A golden test set is the most valuable asset a team builds. It holds hundreds of real, de-identified call scenarios, each with a known right answer, and it runs three times on every change to models, prompts, tools or personas.

Common guidance, here from the vendor Hamming's evaluation guide, is 200 or more scenarios before production and 500 or more for multilingual enterprise use.[27] vendor-reported Detecting a 5-point change in a pass rate near 80% takes several hundred runs per version, not the 100 sometimes quoted. author's arithmetic The set should mix calls that go to plan with edge cases, errors, adversarial callers and noisy audio, and every production failure should join it as a new case. A drop in score then blocks a release, the same way failing tests block code.

FOUNDATION
The agent has to pick out the caller's voice

Real callers phone from cars, kitchens and waiting rooms, with a TV on or someone talking beside them. How the agent performs there matters more than how it performs in a quiet demo.

In one vendor test, by ai-coustics, background speech pushed the error rates of streaming speech-to-text to between 22% and 31%, and isolating the main speaker cut errors by up to 43%.[9] vendor-reported

The remedy is diarization, which separates the voices so that the agent can lock onto the caller and verify identity again if a different voice takes over before a sensitive step.[28] For the same reason, tests should run at several noise levels and with side conversations, not just on clean audio.

EXPERIMENT
Emotion scores are a signal, not a judgment

During the call, frustration or confusion should change how the agent speaks or trigger a warm handoff, a transfer that carries the context with it. After the call, sentiment scored on every call replaces quality reviews that sample a few percent.

An emotion score is best treated as a signal for routing and review, not a judgment of the caller. Accuracy varies, so a team should check that the scores work equally across accents and age groups. In the EU, people must be told when an emotion recognition system is used.[10]

EXPERIMENT
The persona is the brand

Callers judge the organization by how its agent sounds. Voice, pace, warmth and word choice deserve the same care as a brand guide.

One subscription business reported resolution rates up more than 50% after tuning its voice persona, according to Sierra.[14] vendor-reported I would offer callers a choice of a few personas and then keep each caller's preferred voice consistent on every call. author's view Every persona says it is an AI, never implies a professional license, and gives the same facts and policies.[29]

The two experiments have little evidence behind them so far. What exists is mostly vendor case studies, and none yet shows emotion detection or personas improving AI-agent outcomes at scale. author's view The foundations are the place to start, and the next section does the same sorting for the calls themselves.

8 · TRUST AND IDENTITY

A voice is no longer proof of identity

Voices can now be cloned, so an identity check has to rise with the harm that a wrong answer could do.

Over half

Pindrop reports that over half of fraud attempts at health care contact centers now involve AI-generated elements. They include synthetic voice, bots and reconnaissance through the phone menu (the interactive voice response system, or IVR).[4] vendor-reported

Identity on the phone is a brand question as much as a security one. Customers will be targeted by callers pretending to be the organization, and its agent will hear callers pretending to be customers.[30] A voiceprint alone can be fooled: in 2023 a reporter got into his own bank account with an AI clone of his voice.[31]

Heavier checks have costs too. Liveness checks reject some genuine callers over phone-quality audio, and texted codes lose some older or distressed callers. That is why the controls below always keep a person as the fallback. author's view

Six controls put the strongest checks on the requests that can do the most harm. They are my recommendations.
Run liveness and deepfake detection in the contact center platform, before any protected data is disclosed.
Step up verification with a one-time code to the number on file; a birth date or security questions alone are not enough.
For payments, prescription changes and other authorizations, add voiceprint verification with liveness detection.
Match the friction to the risk: passive signals for status checks, a one-time code for account changes, and voiceprint plus liveness only for irreversible actions. Always offer a person as the fallback.
Publish a "we will never ask you for" policy, and make marketing its owner.
In the US, outbound AI-voice calls have counted as artificial-voice calls under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA) since the FCC's 2024 ruling, so they generally need prior express consent. Health care exemptions are narrow, so reminder programs should be checked with counsel.[32] Consent belongs to the current subscriber, so outbound lists should be checked against the FCC Reassigned Numbers Database before every campaign.[12]

9 · WHAT COMES NEXT

The next step is a model that looks facts up

The research on such models is early, but the fact store they depend on is worth building now.

Zhao and colleagues proposed the Limited Memory Language Model (LMLM) in a paper at the ICLR 2026 conference. It is trained so that specific facts, such as a plan's coverage or a card's limit, live in an external database and not in the model's weights. During training the model is not rewarded for memorizing those values; it learns to issue a lookup and copy the answer. Language and reasoning stay in the model, and facts stay in a table that people can inspect, correct and delete.[11]

The approach suits slow-changing knowledge such as benefits, policies and fees. Live data such as balances, claims and appointment slots stays on tool and MCP calls. The demo below deletes a customer's record and shows what each kind of model then says.

Once the record is deleted, the limited-memory model can no longer give the answer, while the standard model goes on repeating it from its weights. The demo mirrors the paper's unlearning result. Removing records from the store made the model forget them with no loss of general ability, where gradient-based unlearning, which adjusts a model's weights to make it forget, degraded it.[11] illustrative

A governed fact store fits regulated conversations

Once facts sit in a store they become governed data: each one is a row with access control, versioning and change review. Every answer has provenance, because each lookup is a logged call with the value it returned. Deletion from the model is real, since removing rows forgets them without retraining.[11] Copies in logs, transcripts, caches and backups still need their own deletion and retention rules.

Less sensitive data sits in the weights, which matters because researchers have extracted names, phone numbers and emails from model weights, even from data seen once.[33][34] There is also room to shrink the model. In research, a limited-memory model with 382M parameters came close to a 7B model on factual precision; the details are in the technical appendix (T6).[11]

The fact store is the safe investment, and training is a gated bet

Retrieval on a frontier model, one of the largest general-purpose language models, is the safe default today. The evidence for limited-memory models is still research-stage: Wikipedia facts, models under 400M parameters, and no dialog, voice or production use. A governed fact store pays off either way, so I would build it now and treat training as a bet to stage behind gates. author's view

11 · A PILOT PLAYBOOK

Set the gates before launch and let day 90 decide

A 90-day pilot settles the question only if the conditions for scaling and for stopping are written down before the first live call.

Teams are at different points on this path. For a team that is starting out, expanding to new call types or resetting a stalled rollout, this is one way to run a pilot. For a team already in production, the golden test set, the gates and the kill criteria work as an audit of what it has.

The shape is narrow by design: one market or line of business, inbound calls only, two or three call types, and a warm transfer to a person with full context. The agent takes no live call until it clears a gate at the end of week 4.

The pilot runs in four steps, from the first test scenario to the decision on day 90. The schedule and the bar are my proposal.
Weeks 0 to 4
Build the golden test set

The set holds 200 or more scenarios from the team's own de-identified calls: accents, older callers, Spanish, number dictation, mid-call corrections, background noise and chatter, and upset callers. The agent is then tested silently, in shadow mode, on recorded audio.

Gate, end of week 4
Risk sets the pass^3 bar

The bar is about 85% for informational calls and 95% for anything that writes data or moves money, with zero critical failures at any rate. Every scenario is run three times on phone-grade audio. Router thresholds are calibrated on held-out data, and identity controls are signed off.

Weeks 5 to 12
Go live in one market

Every failure becomes a regression test. Tool-call write-backs and misroutes (transfers after routing) are audited every week.

T1 · ARCHITECTURE

Three generations, converging on "talk fast, think slow"

The newest voice systems pair a fast conversational layer with a slower reasoning one. The older cascade still offers the most control.

Voice agents have been built in three ways. A cascade chains three models: speech-to-text (STT) transcribes the caller, a large language model (LLM) writes the reply and text-to-speech (TTS) speaks it. Native speech-to-speech replaces the chain with one model that takes audio in and gives audio out. Continuous full-duplex goes a step further, with a model that listens and speaks at the same time.

The newest systems share one design: talk fast, think slow. OpenAI's GPT-Live removes the separate turn detector and delegates search and reasoning to a frontier model behind an asynchronous boundary, so a slow tool call cannot stall audio.[26][37] Sierra's Tandem Voice pairs a real-time conversation layer with a reasoning layer,[14] and Gemini 3.8 Live calls tools in the background.[38] A System-1 router, a small model that returns typed decisions and never writes prose, fits beside any of these as the dispatch layer.

A cascade gives the most control and text at every step, while full-duplex handles turns itself and offers the least control today. The table is my summary of the announcements and documentation cited, not a set of measurements.

T2 · LATENCY BUDGET

Even with a fast router, a cascade lands near 1.5 seconds a turn

People leave about a quarter of a second between turns, and a cascade cannot be pushed much below a second and a half without clipping callers.

Across ten languages, the gaps between speakers' turns cluster around a quarter second.[40] independent Measured platform turns in Cekura's benchmark, which a testing vendor runs, averaged 1.27 s to 3.08 s.[2] vendor-reported

The trade-off deserves candor. A cascade takes turns like a walkie-talkie: it waits for silence, transcribes, reasons and only then speaks. Even with a fast router and prefetch it lands near 1.5 s a turn, and cutting endpointing (the wait for silence that ends a turn) any further clips callers mid-sentence. A team that needs turns under 800 ms as a hard requirement should use a speech-to-speech or full-duplex model and accept a coarser, after-the-fact audit trail. The thinker fits beside either.

T3 · MEASURING RELIABILITY

Single-run scores flatter every vendor

An agent that completes a scenario once may fail it on the next call, and a single-run score hides that.

Repeat reliability, written pass^k, counts a scenario only when all k of its runs succeed. The measure came from Sierra's tau-bench and now appears in Cekura Bench and in the voice mode of tau2-bench. Even if the runs are independent, small per-call error rates compound quickly. At 94.0% per call, only 83.1% of scenarios would pass three for three, assuming independent runs. Across 30,000 calls a month, that per-call rate still means about 1,800 failed calls.

Two findings complicate even this measure. The first is that a score depends on the test setup, or harness, and on the exact model. In Cekura's speech-to-speech benchmark (11 models, 2,952 calls), GPT Realtime 2.1 alone reached 79.3% pass^3, while its mini sibling scored 50.0%. The mini model also saved all fields correctly on only 64.5% of data-saving calls, versus 92.0%.[42] vendor-reported

The second is that recovery is its own axis. A 2026 interruption benchmark found task completion and recovery quality only partly related.[43] Barge-in, when the caller interrupts the agent, and correction should therefore be tested separately from happy paths.

Perception is measured by word error rate (the share of words transcribed wrongly) on phone-grade audio, by accent and language, and on noisy, multi-speaker recordings (a TV, a car, a family member talking). Entity accuracy is measured for names, dates, and phone and account numbers.

Emotion detection is checked on labeled calls for frustration and confusion caught in real time, across accents and age groups. Sentiment is scored on every call after it ends.
Persona is tested on the same golden set: each persona passes it, and tone may differ but facts and policies may not.

When a voice agent fails, the language model is seldom where the trouble starts. A television in the room is transcribed as the caller, the agent answers its own echo, or a correctly transcribed phone number reaches the tool as a different value. The evidence is uneven: it comes from vendor benchmarks, a public echo test by webrtcHacks, and research papers.

Section 5 argued for a thinker-responder design, in which a fast System-1 thinker decides silently and a conversational LLM speaks. Checked against the evidence, the thesis divides into five parts.

Two parts of the thesis hold, two need a qualification and one needs a guardrail. The verdicts are my reading of the studies cited on each line. author's view
THE THINKER-RESPONDER THESIS, CHECKED
HOLDS
Routing a call to a sub-agent or toolset is closed-set classification, a choice from a fixed list. Small decision models do it in roughly 20 to 250 ms for fractions of a cent, versus about 2 s for a frontier LLM.[8]
HOLDS
Narrowing tools first helps. In one study, retrieving the right tools before selection lifted selection accuracy from 13.6% to 43.1% and cut prompt tokens by more than half. Anthropic reports similar gains once agents have 10 or more tools.[58][59]
QUALIFY
For a fixed intent list, a plain fine-tuned classifier is often both the fastest and the most accurate router. Hosted decision APIs earn their place when intents change often or labeled data is scarce.[52][57]
QUALIFY
Decision models are not uniformly "great at decisions." Published accuracy for the same model on the same benchmark varies by more than 10 points across labs, and benign-looking context flipped about 61% of correct decisions in one study.[55][48]
GUARDRAIL
Fraud, clinical concern, complaints and vulnerable-customer signals escalate by rule, never by a threshold. Multi-intent calls ("overcharged again and nobody calls back") need multi-label output.[47]

Routing also chooses tools, which an agent reaches through Model Context Protocol (MCP) servers. Anthropic reports that 58 tools across 5 servers consume about 55K tokens before a conversation starts, and that tool search lifted accuracy from 49% to 74% on one model.[59] On one benchmark, MCP-Zero selected from about 2,800 tools across 308 servers with 98% fewer tokens.[60] Retrieval recall then becomes the new bottleneck: with 4,027 tools, the right tool reached the top results only 56% to 64% of the time.[61] The router design I recommend builds on these findings.

The design has ten parts, from the classifier to the audit log and the compliance terms. author's view
RECOMMENDED ROUTER DESIGN
Tier 0 is a fine-tuned encoder or a self-hosted decision model with multi-label output (one sigmoid per intent), so that "billing and complaint" returns both. In bilingual markets it should start from a multilingual encoder such as XLM-R and be tested on turns that mix languages, because English-only encoders misroute them.
The stop control lets the thinker flush and mute the audio output immediately when it flags risk while the agent is already speaking, and then hand off.
Calibration uses temperature scaling, a one-parameter rescaling of the model's probabilities, on the team's own data. Per-intent thresholds are set on a held-out set, never on the test set.
The gate sends a turn that is below threshold, out of scope or carrying two high-stakes intents to a frontier model with the full schema, or to a person.
Hard rules always escalate fraud, clinical concern, complaints and vulnerable-customer signals.
Narrow tools mean that each sub-agent exposes only its own MCP servers, with a fallback to broader tools when retrieval scores are low.
Injection hygiene means that the router classifies the caller's turn only, with no retrieved or third-party text in context.[48]
State lives in one call-state object per call, held by the orchestrator. The thinker runs on every finished turn and writes typed fields (intent, risk, speaker, emotion, confidence). The responder owns the conversation, slot filling and clarifying questions ("does Dr. Smith work Fridays?"), and reads the thinker's fields before it speaks. A new intent, topic shift or correction triggers a re-route; slots already filled carry over.
The audit log records the input hash, label, probabilities, threshold, model version and final disposition, and evaluations are re-run on every model change.
Compliance depends on hosting: a hosted decision API needs a BAA or equivalent for protected data, and a self-hosted encoder avoids the question.

The comparison points to a division of labor: a classifier for stable, high-volume intents, and a decision API or an LLM for new, rare or out-of-scope ones. T6 turns from how an agent decides to where it keeps its facts.

T6 · LIMITED MEMORY LANGUAGE MODELS

What the limited-memory research shows, and what it does not

In its authors' tests, a model with 382M parameters that looks facts up came close to one with 7B on factual accuracy. There is no evidence yet on dialog, speech, models above about 400M or enterprise data.

Section 9 introduced the Limited Memory Language Model (LMLM), which keeps facts in an external store that people can inspect, correct and delete, and not in its weights. The research behind it is a paper by Zhao et al., "Pre-training Limited Memory Language Models with Internal and External Knowledge," at ICLR 2026. Its code and its 176M and 382M checkpoints are public under the MIT license.[11][35]

In training, the text is annotated with inline lookups: an entity, a relation and then the retrieved value. The training loss is zeroed on the tokens of the retrieved value. The model still trains on every other token, so it learns when and how to look up, not the value itself. The store holds 54.6M (entity, relation, value) triples, extracted from Wikipedia by an 8B annotator trained on GPT-4o-labeled seeds.

At inference a lookup token pauses generation, the query is matched by embedding similarity (with a rejection threshold of 0.6) and the value is inserted. The training budget was about 3B tokens, 8 epochs and at most 8 H100-days per model.

With its store, the 382M LMLM scores close to the 7B LLaMA2 on FactScore and T-REx and well above it on PopQA; without the store, it falls below the standard 382M model. The scores, on three tests of factual accuracy, are from Zhao et al.[11] EM stands for exact match.

The rival approach is retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), in which a standard model is handed retrieved text when it answers. In the same paper, a 355M RAG baseline scored higher on T-REx than the same-size LMLM (75.8 vs 58.7) but lower on FactScore and PopQA, so retrieval-augmented frontier models remain the practical benchmark to beat.[11]

A follow-up paper in July 2026, Co-LMLM, replaces the relational triples with vector keys and free-text values, so knowledge is no longer limited to entity attributes. A retrieval takes about 2.2 ms, which fits inside a voice turn, against 28.6 ms for a lookup that generates a question. Its authors also report that lookup timing is imperfect: forcing lookups added 5.9 points on SimpleQA.[17]

How much of a model is fact storage? The research answers only indirectly, by showing how much smaller a model can be once it retrieves.

Retrieval models have matched models 18 to 50 times larger on knowledge-heavy tasks. The reductions are those reported for LMLM, RETRO and Atlas.[11][62][63]

Six things have to be true before a regulated contact center can rely on a limited-memory model. author's view
WHAT HAS TO BE TRUE FOR REGULATED CONTACT CENTERS
Extraction has to be accurate. Turning policy text and records from customer (CRM) and health record (EHR) systems into rows relies on LLM annotators, and their errors become confident wrong answers.
The store has to hold more than entity facts. Contact center knowledge is often procedural ("if the plan is X and the claim is Y"). Free-text values help, but that is unproven here.
Lookups have to be reliable. The model must know when to look up and must not fall back on its weights.
Live data still needs tools. Balances and appointments change by the minute, and an LMLM covers static knowledge, not transactions.
The total cost has to beat RAG. Pre-training a private small model, plus building and refreshing the store, must beat retrieval on a frontier API.
The evidence has to reach scale. There is nothing yet on dialog, speech, models above about 400M or enterprise data. A 2026 position paper argues for this direction but reports no deployments.[66]

So far the research shows a mechanism that works at small scale on encyclopedia facts, which is why section 9 proposes testing it in stages. T7 turns to where models and data run.

The nine unverified or wrong claims should not be repeated as fact, and the six partial ones need their caveat attached. T11 turns from claims to practice.

T11 · TOOLING AND IMPLEMENTATION

By one vendor's account, implementations fail on vague goals and missing handoffs

A published account of failed chatbot projects points to goals, integration, handoff, knowledge and measurement.

The last question for an engineering team is what to borrow and what to avoid. On the frontend, Vercel's AI Voice Elements give React components for persona, microphone capture, transcript and playback.[84] They are useful for web prototypes and irrelevant to telephony, which is how a contact center's calls arrive.

Netguru's account of why chatbot implementations fail says what to avoid: abstract goals instead of concrete use cases, bots isolated from scheduling and CRM systems, no handoff path, messy knowledge and no measurement after launch.[85] vendor-reported OpenAI's own rebuild adds a lesson on testing: it relied on a silent production test to find failures that synthetic load tests had missed.[37][86]

Both accounts fit the argument of this report: what separates the teams that succeed is not which model they buy.
